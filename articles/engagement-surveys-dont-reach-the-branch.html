Engagement surveys don't reach the branch

One in five workers worldwide is engaged. The bigger question is who got asked.

RC
Rahul ChatterjeeAuthor of EmoShift19 November 2026 · 3 min read

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report puts global employee engagement at 20% in 2025 - the lowest since 2020, down from a 23% peak in 2022, costing the world economy an estimated $10 trillion in lost productivity. Numbers like that send every HR team back to its engagement survey.

In the microfinance company where I head learning and development, about 11,000 of our 13,000 people work in the field, spread across roughly 3,000 branches. When I read a global engagement number, my first question is not what it says. It is who got counted - because in organisations like ours, the annual engagement survey barely reaches the branch.

How the survey misses the field

Look at how a typical survey travels. It is designed at head office, in English, as a link. It assumes a personal email address, a personal phone, and ten quiet minutes. A field officer has none of these reliably. He is on the road from morning, his data pack is for work apps, and the survey link arrives in a WhatsApp group between two collection reminders.

So the responses skew. Head office and back office answer in full. Branch staff answer partly, late, or not at all - or they answer with the branch manager standing nearby. What comes back is called the company score. Very often, it is a head-office score with a field garnish.

The number looks fine. That is the problem.

The dangerous outcome is not a bad score. It is a reassuring one. The dashboard shows engagement a point up from last year, the board note goes out, and nobody asks how many of the 11,000 field officers are inside that number.

A score the field barely answered cannot tell you the field is engaged. It can only tell you the people with desks are. Meanwhile the resignation that surprises everyone still comes from a branch - from a person who was never really asked.

What I would change

A few shifts I would argue for:

1. Take the survey to the branch, not the branch to the survey. A short, vernacular survey, administered on branch time - ten protected minutes in a branch meeting - will beat a longer link that dies in a WhatsApp group.

2. Report the branch its own results. A branch that never sees its score learns that the survey is something done to it, not for it. One page, in the branch's language, discussed openly, changes what people say next year.

3. One visible action per branch. Not a programme with a name and a budget - one thing the branch raised, fixed where the branch can see it. Nothing teaches people that surveys matter like watching one complaint turn into one change.

4. Measure managers on follow-through, not participation. Chasing response rates produces obedient clicking. A branch manager asked "what did your branch say, and what did you do?" produces answers worth reading.

The emotion under the score

The EmoShift lens applies here. Behind a low response rate sits an emotion, not apathy - usually futility: the learned belief that answers go into a black hole, and that a frank answer may travel back to the branch manager. An officer who believes that does not sabotage the survey. He does something worse. He clicks politely.

An officer who believes nobody is reading does not sabotage the survey. He clicks politely.

I cannot prove that better branch surveying lifts engagement. Engagement is never one thing. But I have watched what people write when they finally believe someone is reading - and it is nothing like what they click when they do not.

One question for you

When your last engagement survey closed, what share of your frontline actually answered - and did anyone in a branch ever see what they said? I would like to hear.

Promoted for results, not prepared: the field officer who becomes a branch manager

A promotion letter changes a title. It does not teach anyone to manage people.

RC
Rahul ChatterjeeAuthor of EmoShift20 October 2026 · 4 min read

A promotion letter does not teach anyone how to manage people.

Take a capable field officer who becomes a branch manager. Yesterday the job was to deliver results. Today it is to help a team deliver them. Most organisations treat that as a change of title and move on.

Let me tell you about someone who did it differently. Some years ago, during one of my banking assignments, a colleague came to me before applying for a branch manager role through an internal job posting. I was heading training there at the time, and we talked through what the job would really demand. He applied, and he qualified.

What stayed with me is what he did next. He kept learning, and he kept discussing his areas of improvement and his challenges with his supervisors and seniors. About a year and a half later, he prepared for the next level, a cluster-head role, in the same way: he sought advice, applied, and qualified again. Today he is a business head in a microfinance company, thriving in his career.

What the data says, and what it does not

Gallup's 2026 global workplace report puts manager engagement at 22% in 2025, down from 27% in 2024 and 31% in 2022. That is global data across all sectors, not a finding about microfinance. Read the Gallup summary.

A separate Gallup post says most frontline supervisors are promoted for performance or years in a frontline role, not for supervisory skill. It reports that supervisors promoted that way are less engaged than those chosen for supervisory experience, 31% against 42%. See the Gallup post. I read this as a reason to look at how we prepare new managers, not as proof about any one company.

Promotion changes a title. Practice builds a manager.

What a new branch manager actually faces

After twenty years in BFSI learning and development, I have come to judge a manager programme by what happens after the workshop. Three tests are enough:

1. Can they correct a mistake without humiliating the person? A field officer who is corrected in front of the team stops asking questions.

2. Can they tell what the person needs? Sometimes it is practice. Sometimes it is clearer priorities. Sometimes it is simply someone who listens.

3. Can they talk about targets without every conversation sounding like a warning?

Build the first 90 days around real work

I would not give a new branch manager a course and a certificate. I would give them three things to do, with someone watching:

A difficult conversation, rehearsed before it happens. A coaching conversation, observed and discussed afterwards. A short weekly check-in with an experienced manager, kept for the full 90 days.

Measure it after the workshop

Do not count course completions. Ask whether repeat errors are falling, whether people ask for help earlier, and whether customer conversations are improving. Those are things a branch can see within a quarter.

In my own thinking, the EmoShift lens starts here: notice the emotion before you choose the response. A new manager who can do that under target pressure has learned the hardest part.

Try this next month

Pick one newly promoted manager. Agree one difficult conversation they are avoiding. Rehearse it for ten minutes, let them have it, then ask how it went.

What is one people-management skill you wish you had practised before you led your first team? I would like to hear it.

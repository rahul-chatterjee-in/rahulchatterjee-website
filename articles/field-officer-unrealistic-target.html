Why a field officer won't say the target is unrealistic

A number set in a head office meeting can be impossible in a village. The person who knows stays quiet.

RC
Rahul ChatterjeeAuthor of EmoShift12 November 2026 · 3 min read

In the microfinance company where I head learning and development, about 11,000 of our 13,000 people work in the field. Every month, every one of them receives a number. And almost none of them will ever say the number is unrealistic - even when it is.

That silence costs more than any missed target. It is worth understanding where it comes from.

How a target travels

A target begins as arithmetic: the growth the company needs, divided by regions, divided by branches. On its way down - head office to region, region to area, area to branch, branch to officer - each level adds a small cushion, because no manager wants to promise exactly what was asked and no more.

By the time the number reaches the field officer, it has passed through four or five hands. None of those hands knows his village. The officer knows which borrowers are seasonal, which crop failed, which festival emptied the market this month. The target knows none of this. And the one person with ground knowledge was never in the room when the number was set.

Why he stays quiet

Watch what happens to the officer who says a target is unrealistic. The word he hears back is rarely "unrealistic". It is "capability". Or "commitment". In most branches, questioning the number is quietly read as weakness, and the officer who questions it twice is marked.

So the rational officer absorbs the number and copes in silence. Silence is not agreement. Most of the time, it is fear drawing a salary.

What the silence costs

The cost does not look like laziness. It looks like coping. Next month's pipeline gets quietly under-reported so the next target starts lower. Branch numbers get dressed up for the review call. Worst of all, the pressure travels one more step down - to the borrower - when any loan starts to look better than the right loan.

Then comes the resignation that surprises head office. It should not surprise anyone. The officer lived with the gap between the number and the ground for months. He just never found a safe way to say so. And every month he stayed quiet, the reports head office read described effort, not reality.

What I would change

I keep returning to an exit interview from our own company. A leaver told us that in 90 days, nobody asked about his well-being. He was given targets, and when he missed, he was questioned - but nobody helped him hit them.

That interview changed how I think about the conversation around targets. A few shifts I would argue for:

1. Ask "what would make this achievable?" before "why did you miss?" The first question produces information. The second produces excuses.

2. Test the number against the ground before it is final. A target discussed with the officer who must deliver it is not a softer target. It is a more honest one.

3. Measure managers on helping, not only on questioning. A branch manager who helps an officer hit a number does more for retention and performance than one who audits the miss.

4. Coach hardest in the first 90 days, when the gap between the number and the ground is newest and the officer has not yet learned that silence is safer.

The emotion under the silence

The EmoShift lens applies here. Under a field officer's silence sits an emotion - usually fear: of being judged, of being transferred, of being the name that comes up when numbers are discussed. A manager who notices the emotion will ask one more question. A manager who reads silence as agreement will walk away informed of nothing.

I cannot prove that silence around targets is what pushes people out of a company. Attrition is never one thing. But I have sat in enough exit interviews to notice how clearly people speak once they finally feel safe - and to wonder what they would have said six months earlier, if someone had asked the right question.

One question for you

When did someone on your frontline last tell you a target was unrealistic - and what happened to them for saying it? I would like to hear.

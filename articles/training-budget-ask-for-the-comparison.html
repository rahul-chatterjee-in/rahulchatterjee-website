Training budgets

Before you fund the next training programme, ask for the comparison

A decision memo from the field: four questions before the money moves.

RC
Rahul ChatterjeeAuthor of EmoShift26 November 2026 · 4 min read

To: The leader about to sign a training budget

From: Rahul Chatterjee

Re: Four questions before the money moves

If collections improved after a training programme, would I ask the CFO to credit the course? Not yet. I would first ask what changed in the branches that did not receive it.

I head learning and development in a microfinance company, so I sit on both sides of this conversation. I am the person asking for the budget, and I am the person who has to defend it when the results arrive. Over the years I have learned that the most dangerous moment for a training budget is not the sceptical question. It is the good story: the branch that improved right after the programme, the officer who says the course changed how she works. Stories like that can be true and still not be evidence. The branch may have improved because a new manager arrived, because the season turned, or because the officer was already your best and finally got invited to something.

This memo is the check I would run before asking anyone to fund a programme. It is four questions. They fit on one card, and they work whether you sign the cheque or request it.

The decision card

1. What business measure are we actually moving?Not attendance, not completion certificates, not satisfaction scores. One business number the programme exists to change: collections, productivity, error rates, early attrition, whatever matters most for this programme. If the room cannot agree on the measure before funding, it will not agree on success afterwards.

2. What is the comparison?Some branches or teams get the programme now; comparable ones get it later. That second group is not a laboratory control. It is simply the honest question: what happened in similar places that did not receive the training? Without a comparison, every improvement belongs to the programme, and so does every coincidence.

3. What else changed?New targets, a new incentive plan, a seasonal upswing, a manager change. In a field organisation, three things change every quarter. Put the plausible ones next to the result, in the same meeting, before anyone celebrates.

4. What evidence would change our mind?Decide in advance what would justify continuing, changing, or stopping. If no possible answer would ever lead to stopping, this is not an evaluation. It is a ceremony.

A worked example (illustrative, not a company result)

Say a microfinance company trains 200 branches on collections conversations. Over the next quarter, the collections rate in those branches rises from 82% to 86% of amounts due. The story writes itself: training works. Now apply the card.

The measure was agreed upfront: the collections rate, not course completion. The comparison exists: 200 similar branches scheduled for training next quarter, whose rate moved from 81% to 83% in the same period. So the trained branches gained four percentage points and the untrained branches two, leaving a two-point difference in the gains. What else changed: the same quarter brought a revised incentive for collections staff, covering both groups. These numbers alone cannot separate the effect of the training from the effect of the incentive, and they do not show which way that tangle bends the two-point gap. And the bar was set in advance: treat the gap as promising only if it holds for two more quarters; redesign the programme if it shrinks; stop it if it vanishes.

The programme may still deserve funding. But the decision now rests on something sturdier than a story.

What this looks like from the branch

A credible comparison in a field organisation has to respect the field. Comparable branches do similar work in similar conditions; a metro branch is not a fair comparison for a rural one. Starting conditions matter; a branch that was already improving will make any programme look good. And the branch's own people will usually tell you what else changed, if someone asks them. The cheapest evaluation instrument I know is a branch manager asked a straight question.

The emotion in the funding meeting

The EmoShift lens applies here too, quietly. A training budget carries emotion on both sides of the table: the L&D head's hope that this programme will be the one that proves the function's worth, and the finance leader's fear of funding faith. A decision card does not remove the emotion. It gives both people something to hold while they feel it.

One offer

Use the four questions as they are, adapt them, or challenge them. If you run a version of this check before your next funding decision, I would like to hear what it surfaced, especially where it failed. I read every reply.
